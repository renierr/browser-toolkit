# SOS and Morse Code

SOS is the most universally recognized distress signal in the world. It doesn't stand for "Save Our Ships"—it was chosen simply because it is extremely distinct and easy to transmit in Morse code.

## The SOS Pattern

The pattern is: **3 short, 3 long, 3 short.**
Pause. 
Repeat.

`• • •   - - -   • • •`

### 1. Using Sound (Whistle or Honking)
- **Short** (.): A fast 1-second blast.
- **Long** (-): A sustained 3-second blast.
- **Pause**: Wait a few seconds between sequences, and 10 seconds before starting the whole sequence over.

### 2. Using Light (Flashlight or Mirror)
- **Short**: Flash the light for 1 second.
- **Long**: Hold the light on for 3 seconds.
- Flashlights with a strobe function are highly visible from the air, but an intended SOS pattern proves human intelligence and distress.

## Visual SOS Markers

If placing signals on a beach, clearing, or in snow:
- Make the letters **S O S** massive. They must be at least 10 feet (3 meters) high to be seen clearly by aircraft.
- Use high-contrast materials: Dark rocks on white snow, or bright clothing on dark earth.
- Use straight lines for the "S" to resemble numeric '5's if rocks are hard to carve curves with.

:::tip
Any signal organized as a **group of three** is internationally recognized as a call for help. (3 fires in a triangle, 3 whistle blasts, 3 gunshots).
:::

:::warning
Do not use SOS lightly or for a "test". Rescuers are legally bound to respond to confirmed SOS signals, which triggers massive emergency mobilization.
:::
